0.664 is a mediocre-sounding number, and it is the wrong measure. No care-management team consumes a probability. They have capacity to work some number of patients a week, so the question that matters is: if we work the top 20% of the list, what do we get? The answer is 37.7% of all readmissions at 1.89× enrichment — and that is a useful list regardless of what the AUC says.

Three models, including a baseline worth beating

A model is only interesting relative to the simplest thing that could work. Here that is a rule: rank by prior inpatient visit count.

ModelROC AUCPR AUCvs base rate Recall @20%Calibration
The logistic model's AUC is fine and its probabilities are useless. It ranks almost as well as the gradient boosting model — AUC 0.650 against 0.664 — but its mean predicted risk is 46.3% against an observed rate of 11.1%, with a Brier score of 0.227 against 0.094. It is off by a factor of four in absolute terms. If anyone took those numbers as probabilities and told a patient "you have a 46% chance of readmission", they would be badly wrong while the ranking still looked healthy. Discrimination and calibration are different properties, and only one of them shows up in an AUC.

Calibration — predicted against observed

A calibration slope of 1.0 and intercept of 0.0 is perfect. Brier is lower-is-better.

Leakage test: does patient grouping matter?

23.3% of patients appear in more than one encounter. Splitting randomly lets the same patient sit in train and test.

Two things I expected to matter that did not, reported anyway.

1. Patient-grouped splitting barely changed anything. Random-split AUC 0.679 against patient-grouped 0.677 for the gradient boosting model — an inflation of 0.0023. Grouping on patient ID is still the correct thing to do, and I did it. But the honest number is that it moved almost nothing here, and reporting it as a dramatic fix would be a lie.

2. Isotonic recalibration made calibration worse. Fitted on half the hold-out patients, the calibration slope went from 0.906 to 0.782, Brier from 0.0942 to 0.0945, and AUC from 0.6491 to 0.6483. The model was already well calibrated; the recalibration had nothing to fix and cost a little. It stayed in the repository as a negative result rather than being quietly deleted.
Limitations. The data is 1999–2008, so coding practice and care pathways have moved on — this is a methods demonstration, not a deployable model. 2,423 encounters were removed where readmission was impossible (deaths and hospice discharges), because leaving them in the denominator flatters the model. Readmissions after 30 days fold into the negative class, since under 30 days is the CMS-penalised event. And before anyone deployed this, the number that matters is not AUC — it is what the care team's current process already catches, which this dataset cannot tell you.
